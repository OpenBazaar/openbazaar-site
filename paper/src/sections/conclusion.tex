\section{Conclusion}
\label{sec:conclusion}

We have described a marketplace in which the public catalog contains only what a seller chooses to publish---hardened by plain-text checks, a closed 15-category taxonomy, per-store signed catalog indexes (\code{OBCX}), and directory admission snapshots (\code{OBAX})---a buyer's shipping address is sealed to one seller and never enters the catalog, filtering of what a buyer sees happens on the buyer's own device, post-purchase return cases stay sealed and decoupled from escrow, payments are non-custodial, and escrow is a two-party Bitcoin key in which a moderator can decide a dispute without ever being able to take the funds. Read-only public browsing lets Tor Browser visitors inspect admitted stores directly from seller onions without a central proxy while keeping checkout and escrow keys out of untrusted web JavaScript; a planned buyer-only light mode extends that trust boundary into signed desktop and mobile apps so buyers need not run an always-on node. The escrow's safety rests on a short list of checks that each party runs before funding: the moderator record is the one it trusts, the heights lie in the future, every payout is standard and above dust, fees fit a floor and a cap and can be bumped, the funding cannot be replaced, and the refund is in hand before anything is signed. The moderator, in turn, binds each ruling to the signed terms and the funding output and never rules twice on one escrow.

The limits are as important as the mechanisms. The seller and the carrier must learn the ship-to. The chain records every payment. A network observer who can watch both ends can correlate timing. A moderator colluding with one party decides the dispute, and a moderator that takes a filing fee and never decides is only shown as overdue. One directory operator chooses the introductions and the fee. The verifiable encryption that keeps the moderator honest costs 50~KB per share. What the design changes is who else learns these things, and that no party can hold the buyer's or seller's funds alone. The most important next steps are shipping the buyer-only light client, completing an external cryptographic review of the escrow protocol (with an independent moderator and a decision on TRUC spends) before enabling mainnet escrow, smaller verifiable encryption, and supporting more than one directory.
